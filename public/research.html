Why it works this way

A clear next action, a choice of pace, and a way back when you pause.

Kitestring supports planning and focus routines. It is not a treatment or a substitute for professional care. Some planning tools here draw on skills taught in adult ADHD CBT. These app features have not been shown to treat ADHD.

These are reasons for design choices, not promises about your results. The explanations and source details are bundled with the app and readable offline. Opening a paper needs internet access.

What the strengths mean

The rating is for the feature in this app and its adult audience, rather than the quality of a whole research field.

  • Strong: repeated, direct evidence for the feature, audience and useful outcome. None of the features below meets this bar.
  • Moderate: related experiments or parts of a larger program support the direction. There is still a gap between that research and this app.
  • Weak: indirect, preliminary or mixed evidence, or a much shorter version of what was studied. The feature's benefit here is not established.

The choices in the app

Moderate evidence

One next action

Choose one small action to start. Keep the larger task list nearby without having to plan everything first.

Solanto studied a therapist-led, multi-week program with adults diagnosed with ADHD. It included planning and organisation. It did not test an isolated next-action card. [S1]

Weak evidence; app adaptation untested

A sufficient finish you choose

Optionally name what would be enough for this pass. Leave it blank, change it or finish without proving you met it. The app never grades the line.

The structured planning program in S1 provides background, not direct evidence for this criterion. It did not test this field or establish a benefit from it. [S1]

A deliberate stop you can label

Weak, indirect evidence; this app adaptation is untested.

After choosing Stop, you may add a factual label. It does not mean the action is finished and changes no recorded time. Undo or remove it whenever you choose. Ordinary Stop needs no label.

Recording a choice is a design decision, not a measure of attention, effort or recovery. General self-monitoring ideas offer indirect background (S16); no benefit from this particular label has been established.

Weak evidence; app adaptation untested

A separate transition allowance

Optionally leave room to put things away or change activities. Your allowance stays separate from the work guess and recorded focus. It does not run another timer or predict your finish time.

S1 provides planning background, not a test of this control or an optimal allowance. The 0–240 minute range is an interface limit. This app adaptation has no demonstrated benefit. [S1]

Weak evidence

A smaller first block

Choose a short block or use your usual length. A tiny start is an option, not a quota.

Safren's adult skills manual teaches adjustable work chunks. It does not establish an ideal tiny-start duration or a benefit from this button. [S3]

Moderate evidence

Your own if-then cue

Save a situation and a possible next step, such as “After tea, open the draft.” Change or skip it when plans change.

Reviews cover cue-response plans across varied goals, and mental contrasting combined with planning. These are different interventions; a single optional field does not inherit their results. [S5] [S6]

Moderate evidence

Park a thought, then decide

Save a distracting thought and decide what to do with it later. Review is optional, and resuming needs no explanation.

Safren's manual pairs recording distractions during an agreed work period with reviewing them afterwards. This supports the sequence, not a separate benefit from a capture box. Urgent needs need no delay. [S3]

Weak evidence

Keep your next step ready

Keep your next step ready for when you return. You can pause without writing a note.

Nonclinical interruption experiments studied preparation and ready-to-resume plans. They do not establish how quickly someone will return using this app. [S11] [S13]

Moderate evidence for recovery

Flexible blocks and breaks

Use work blocks and breaks that fit your day. Brief breaks may help with fatigue. Research has not established an ideal ADHD Pomodoro schedule.

A micro-break review covered varied nonclinical samples; overall performance improvement was not established. A one-day student study compared scheduled and self-regulated breaks. Neither establishes a universal 25/5 ratio. [S8] [S9]

Weak evidence

Remaining, elapsed or hidden time

Choose how much time information you want to see.

An adult ADHD review found a small, varied body of time-perception research. It did not test visual timers or show which display works best. This is a user choice. [S10]

Moderate evidence

Quiet sessions, chosen reminders

Start quietly and choose the reminders you want.

General attention and work experiments studied phone notifications and interruptions. They do not establish an ideal reminder dose for adults with ADHD. Fewer reminders will not suit everyone. [S14] [S15]

Moderate evidence

Records you can hide

Recorded time shows timer use. It does not measure attention. Hide stats when you prefer.

A review of randomized studies examined monitoring progress across varied goals. It does not validate a Focus Score or infer effort, mood or recovery from session minutes. [S16]

Weak evidence

Return without a penalty

Your earlier sessions stay here when you miss a day.

Self-compassion interventions and consumer studies of displayed streaks are different from a neutral return screen. Neither establishes a clinical benefit from interface wording. Keeping history is a product choice. [S17] [S18]

Weak evidence

A self-chosen reward

Add an optional small reward to your action. Rest and breaks remain available regardless of completion.

A health-gamification review found mixed results and limits in study quality. It does not validate this reward field or establish an ADHD-specific benefit. [S19]

Weak evidence for adult daily work

Sound or silence

Try sound or silence. Keep what works for this task. Stop a sound if it distracts you.

White and pink noise studies mostly involved young people with ADHD or high symptoms doing lab tasks. This does not establish a benefit for adult work; the review found no brown-noise studies. Music findings vary by task, including interference. A modulated-music study used nonclinical experiments and had company interests; it does not validate this sound library. Brown noise and binaural beats remain preference options without an established benefit here. [S24] [S25] [S26]

Weak evidence for the brief guide

An optional breathing break

Optional breathing practice. Stop if it feels uncomfortable. An ordinary break is always available.

A structured-breathing study used five minutes daily for a month, not one sigh. A broader review had study-quality limits; another trial found no advantage for one slow rate over a matched comparison. None establishes an instant effect from this guide. [S27] [S28] [S29]
