Monolith or microservices?

Short answer: a modular monolith. One codebase and one web build, cut into clean modules inside. Three programs serve that build. The companion serves dist/ on this Mac. Wrangler uploads it. electron-builder packs the same dist/ into the app. Those three are adapters. They don't count as services in the microservice sense.

The two words

A monolith is one program that does everything. You build it once and ship it once. Parts talk to each other by calling functions, which is instant and can't fail because of the network.

Microservices split the app into many small programs, each with its own deploy and often its own database: a "timer service", a "tasks service", a "stats service". They talk over the network with HTTP or queues.

Why a monolith fits this app

  • One developer, one user. Microservices solve a people problem: 50 engineers stepping on each other in one codebase. You don't have that problem.
  • The timer can't wait for a network. If "start timer" had to ask a tasks service which task is current, a slow Wi-Fi moment could delay your session. In one program the answer comes back in microseconds.
  • It works offline. A plane, a dead café Wi-Fi, a NextDNS block: the app keeps working because nothing it needs is on the other side of a network.
  • It costs nothing. One static bundle plus one tiny Worker stays inside every free tier. Ten services would each want hosting, logs and monitoring.

"Modular" is the part that matters

A monolith can still be tidy. Inside src/ the code is cut by job, and the cuts are real:

FolderJobRule it follows
src/state/timerEngine.tsTimer mathsPure functions. Time goes in, a new state comes out. No React, no audio, no storage, which is why it's easy to test.
src/state/The app's factsStores for settings, timer, tasks, stats. Most facts change here. Notes, mixes, radio and custom backgrounds live elsewhere.
src/lib/PlumbingIndexedDB, the store, backup checking, cloud sync. Backup checking does know task shape, including etaMin, timer segments, and stats chunks named focus, short and long. The rest of the folder is not about how a Pomodoro runs.
src/audio/SoundWeb Audio graph, noises, alerts, radio. Mixes live in mixer.ts. Radio favourites live in radio.ts.
src/ui/The screenReact components. They read stores and call actions. Notes are the exception: NotepadPanel.tsx writes them.

Custom background images live in src/data/themes.ts. The image bytes sit in their own IndexedDB keys, named custom-bg:<id>.

These cuts are why two Codex jobs could work in parallel today: one owned src/, the other desktop/. They met at window.desktop, which has three calls: setStatus, attention and release. Main also sends desktop:prepare-toggle into the page, so a menu click can press Space.

So what are the companion, Worker and Electron?

They're separate programs, so a strict person could call this "a monolith plus three services". The difference from microservices is that none of them owns a slice of the business logic. Each one is an adapter: it gives the same frontend a capability from its surroundings.

  • MAC Companion: hands out the files on localhost and rings native alarms.
  • CLOUDFLARE Worker: answers /api/me and /api/sync, and stores sync copies. With run_worker_first set to ["/api/*"], HTML, JS and sounds are served by the assets layer. The script does not run for those paths.
  • MAC Electron shell: hands out the files inside an app window and adds the menu bar.

Take any adapter away and the app still runs; you just lose that one extra. That's the test: in microservices, losing the "tasks service" means you have no tasks.

When this would change

If the app grew into a product with thousands of users, shared team rooms and payments, you'd probably split off the parts with different scaling or security needs (billing, realtime rooms) first. Even then most teams start with a modular monolith and pull out services only when a real pain shows up.
